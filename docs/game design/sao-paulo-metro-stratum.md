# São Paulo Metro Stratum — Design Intent

> **Intent, not status.** This document records an owner-directed Second Gate content concept. It does not assert that the stratum is implemented, mapped, or assigned final floor numbers.

## Core image

At some point after the player has learned to read the Labyrinth as St. Maria reads it, continued descent opens into something unmistakably recognizable to a modern player as the **São Paulo Metro**.

The game does not announce this revelation. To St. Maria, the place belongs to an unknown or ancient civilization whose infrastructure is outside the town's conceptual vocabulary. The player understands more than the setting does.

The central rule is:

> **The player recognizes the Metro. St. Maria does not.**

Do not flatten that dramatic irony by supplying an exposition scene that explains what a metro system is, proves a single chronology, or translates the space into ordinary fantasy lore.

## Ontological ambiguity

The stratum should make the Labyrinth harder to explain rather than solve it.

The Metro may be:

- a buried place;
- a place reached across time;
- a place reached across realities;
- an operating location intersecting the Labyrinth;
- a memory, projection, or other phenomenon;
- something for which none of those descriptions is fully adequate.

The game does not need to settle this in Second Gate.

A modern passerby or lost present-day NPC may appear. The strongest version is mundane rather than explanatory: somebody may behave like an ordinary commuter or passerby and be entirely unaware that the player considers the location a dungeon. Exact interaction rules, what such people perceive, and whether they are lost at all remain unresolved.

Do not automatically turn these encounters into a dimensional-crossover questline.

## St. Maria's language

Characters from St. Maria should describe what they can infer from the material evidence available to them.

They may understand concepts such as:

- underground roads or passages;
- paired iron rails;
- platforms;
- gates or turnstile-like barriers;
- inscriptions they cannot contextualize;
- machines, lights, doors, signals, and vehicles as artifacts of an unknown people.

They should not casually use modern categories such as **metro**, **subway**, **commuter rail**, or other vocabulary their culture has no reason to possess.

The comedy and unease come from reasonable wrong interpretations, not from making the people of St. Maria stupid.

## Visual direction

Do not over-fantasy the Metro in order to reassure the player that it is still a dungeon.

The stratum should retain enough ordinary contemporary specificity that recognition is immediate:

- tiled platforms and corridors;
- concrete and utilitarian surfaces;
- fluorescent or other institutional lighting;
- safety strips and tactile flooring;
- rails, utility spaces, stairs, escalators, gates, signage, maps, and service infrastructure;
- the spatial rhythm of a functioning urban transit system.

Some spaces may be derelict, impossible, flooded, repurposed, or invaded by the Labyrinth, but at least part of the stratum should feel disturbingly maintained rather than like a picturesque post-apocalyptic ruin.

Exact station identity, real-world branding, route diagrams, logos, and signage are later art/content decisions. Recognition does not require reproducing one named station literally.

## Sound direction

The transition into this stratum should be audible before it is fully legible.

Useful material includes:

- electrical and fluorescent hum;
- ventilation and air pressure;
- escalator machinery;
- warning tones and station chimes;
- public-address fragments;
- distant rail vibration;
- train braking, doors, acceleration, and tunnel resonance.

A train arriving in a space the player has been reading as a dungeon corridor can be a major authored reveal without requiring a cutscene.

## Dungeon integration

The Metro is a **real dungeon stratum**, not a detached joke room or non-canonical bonus map. It must participate in the same expedition, encounter, resource, creature, return, and authored-event logic as the rest of Second Gate unless a specific exception earns its existence.

The stratum should contain its own discoveries, dangers, encounters, and reasons to revisit it. Its contemporary appearance must not make it mechanically disposable.

A natural working placement is in the **middle-to-late game**, after the player has had enough time to internalize St. Maria and the earlier strata. In a twelve-floor working structure, Floors 7–9 are an attractive possible home because the Metro can operate as a third-stratum ontological rupture before the final descent.

That floor assignment is **not frozen**. The durable requirement is that the player first understands what kind of world they think they are in, and only then encounters the Metro.

## Spoiler / marketing rule

This is a high-value discovery and should **not** be part of the default store-page pitch or ordinary first trailer merely because it is visually distinctive.

Marketing may use it later if evidence shows that revealing the stratum helps more than it harms, but the preferred initial experience is for a player to descend through fantasy archaeology and discover contemporary São Paulo unexpectedly.

## Relationship to the Gate corpus

The stratum establishes a useful authorial possibility for the broader Gate series: unmistakable fragments of the creator's lived contemporary world may intrude into otherwise autonomous fantasy spaces without requiring every game to share one exhaustively explained continuity.

Do not therefore repeat the São Paulo Metro mechanically in every Gate. Preserve the stronger principle: **recurrence and impossible recognition can connect the corpus without reducing it to franchise-lore bookkeeping.**
